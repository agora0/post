B: Friend, I know not whether it is my illusion, or whether your narration carries some bias — but why does X sound like a rather arrogant person?

Perhaps this arose from a certain conversation between X and myself. At that time, I had mentioned a myth.

That myth said that in this world (κόσμος = kosmos = world / order / ornament), all the activities of the human soul (ψυχή = psyche) are accompanied by transgression, deviation, and error. In Greek, there is one word that expresses all of these concepts at once — αμαρτία (hamartia = sin / error / missing the mark), a word that carries the meanings of deviation, error, mistake, sin, and transgression together.

The word αμαρτία (hamartia) originates from the notion of "an arrow failing to strike its appointed mark."

B: And then?

A: Upon hearing me say this, X began to laugh, and asked me: then does arrogance count as a transgression? Does arrogance count as the foremost among transgressions?

If a soul (ψυχή = psyche = soul) trespasses at once against the Gods, the world, and fate, then this is indeed transgression and error — that is, αμαρτία (hamartia = sin / error / missing the mark).

Beneath some seemingly inconsequential small talk may well lie hidden a mapping of the truer relations among these adults at the table.

What is interesting is that most adults, in fact, cannot fully perceive whence the content of their own talk arises, for what purpose it exists, or toward what end it will finally move.

If adults are thus, minors are even more so; many of the emotional turbulences of the young are not, in truth, driven by mere restlessness.

The unintended words and unintended slips of children and minors may reflect not merely the ignorant understanding of an immature mind; they may refract the deviations and distortions of the surrounding world itself.

Everyone errs; people's lives are strung together from a series of errors. Errors are perhaps unavoidable, yet people's attitudes toward their own erring differ utterly.

E60 divided people, according to how they treat their errors, into several classes.

The first class: those who know they are erring, who admit their errors to themselves, and who also admit their errors to others.

The second class: those who know they are erring, who admit their errors to themselves, but who will not admit their errors to others.

The third class: those who know they are erring, but who do not admit their errors to themselves, nor admit their errors to others.

The fourth class: those who do not know they are erring, who do not admit their errors to themselves, and who likewise do not admit their errors to others.

This method of classification is rather crude and primitive — but we need not insist on constructing so exhaustive and complete a theory within a mere story.

Perhaps, then, let this be called E60's "Table Theory of Errors."

Every table is a miniature stage upon which people unfold their worlds like arrows in flight, and at the same time, unavoidably, expose their own errors.

According to E60's observation, at any table, people of the first and second classes are always scarce, while people of the third and fourth classes forever constitute the majority.

Knowing one's own errors, admitting those errors to oneself, but never admitting them to others.

These "offerings" representing collective transgression might be animals, or they might be human beings.

After a round of ritual, these living "offerings" might be banished, or they might be put to death.

Once the "offerings" were banished or put to death, the collective "transgression" was thereby ritually, symbolically "expelled."

O,

you dull and hideous world,

what astonishment can you still offer me?

Show me the farce you are capable of;

flaunt no more your stale and tedious so-called miracles.

O7's body remained still, not moving at all,

watching wide-eyed as E60 smeared blood across O7's delicate, beautiful face.

Then the small black cat in O7's arms, with a single claw,

carved a new wound into that hand E60 had stretched toward O7.

E60 began to laugh, somewhat neurotically,

not glancing at the furious little cat,

still staring at O7 like one gone mad.

"Frightened to this degree? Shall I add another flame to the fire? Surely this person will not break?"

E60 thought, savoring the moment within the heart.

"Oh — you are hurt. I had not noticed before… I am sorry."

E60 had already been in a state of half-madness;

upon hearing O7 speak these words softly, in a tone so utterly unexpected,

what remained of E60's reason and control burned instantly to nothing.

In agitation, E60 surged forward and shoved O7 to the ground.

The small cat's fur stood on end all over its body, hissing at E60,

as if ready at any moment to pounce upon this vast demon.

E60 glared savagely at O7,

roaring: "What do you understand! People like you understand nothing!"

That was the first time E60 conceived the thought of killing a person.

Even facing those who bullied E60,

E60 had never once harbored the thought of killing —

yet toward a person who posed no threat and bore no malice, this impulse arose.

E60 had even begun to imagine:

if O7's corpse were sunk into the lake of this sinful school,

how long would it take to be discovered?

In this weather,

what would be the rate of a corpse's decay in water?

This was knowledge E60 had never studied.

O7: Do you need me to accompany you to see a physician?

E60 suppressed the surging emotions with great difficulty.

"Get away! Do not come near me!"

O7, at an utter loss, wore a face full of foolishness, not knowing what to do.

Then E60 watched O7 do something very foolish.

O7 gathered up the bristling little cat,

and said to E60:

"Please do not blame it — it is only a small cat, it understands nothing. It is not usually like this."

For the first time in a long while, E60 felt that peculiar emotion of being provoked into laughter by sheer exasperation.

"This person cannot understand human speech at all."

E60 felt, rather, that there was more common language between E60 and those who bullied E60.

E60: I need no treatment. A wound like this cannot kill me.

O7: You look as though you are in great pain.

E60: You are not me — what the hell would you know.

O7: I am sorry.

E60: Exactly — this is all your fault. Now, take your cat and remove yourself from my sight. Wait — how is it that you remember my name?

O7: Oh — your name sounds beautiful.

E60: What is beautiful about it?

O7: In the ancient tongue it carries the meaning of "liberty." Just like my own name — my name, in the ancient tongue, carries the meaning of "fate."

E60 looked at O7 — upon whose face E60's blood still remained.

E60: And for this alone you remember me?

O7: Every person's name has meaning. My grandmother taught me that when committing another's name to memory, one should hold that name as a kind of blessing.

E60 said provocatively: "I do not like your name."

Yet O7, inexplicably, began to smile: "You do not sound as though you dislike my name as much as you claim."

E60 closed the distance to O7, gazing into O7's eyes.

E60: I truly detest people like you who cannot understand human speech. People of your kind, in this world, mostly do not live long.

O7: May I take this as a kind of blessing?

E60 patted O7's shoulder:

This is no blessing. There are no blessings in this world. Do not be so naive.

Honestly, I have never met anyone as foolish as you.

I hope you will live long and enduringly in this world.

Press onward, student "Fate."
