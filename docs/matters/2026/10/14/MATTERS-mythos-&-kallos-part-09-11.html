My country is a post-revolutionary democratic order (δημοκρατία = democracy), yet such works lack no market there either. Perhaps this too is a part of human nature? Why should it be so? I do not know — but I dearly wish to know.

A: So violence (βία) is beautiful — and the tyrant, being the ultimate incarnation of violence, is therefore beautiful as well?

B: Wait — I most certainly did not lead you into saying that, my friend. I am, after all, a staunch supporter of the democratic order, and I do not believe the tyrant is beautiful. I was merely saying that works which inflame the dark side of human nature lack no market — that is all.

A: But if certain works of art command a great market, does that not signify that, at least within some group, these imaginings (φαντασία = phantasia = imagination) are deemed beautiful? How could people go to behold works of art that are not beautiful?

B: No, no, no — why would you assume that film and television works must equal works of art? And why would you think that works of art must necessarily be beautiful?

A: Is it not so?

B: Of course not! Many film and television works are merely products of a commercialized industrial assembly line; whereas some directors craft works so lofty that few can follow, left only to admire their own solitary fragrance, ending at last in bankruptcy and liquidation. To expand the market, what you need most of the time is not artistry, but catering to people's demands, their pain-points, their comfort-points; what you must truly master is the latent psychological skyline of the people, not some ethereal artistic revelation. So it is with film and television — and so it is with politics.

A appeared profoundly bewildered.

B: What is so hard to understand about this?

A: I truly cannot understand it. Is art not necessarily beautiful?

B: Works of art are truly not necessarily so-called beautiful. Much avant-garde art is so abstract that you cannot comprehend it at all — what beauty is there to speak of? When I look at such works, I only think "this is very artistic," never "this is very beautiful." And certain religious art does nothing but nauseate me — I would never consider that beautiful.

A: My friend, I am deeply perplexed. How can art (τέχνη = techne = art) and beauty (καλός = kalos = beauty) be separated from one another?

B: Good heavens — did you not ask a similar question the very first time we met? I must have felt I was being driven mad by you then, for I feel I am being driven mad by you now. Could you please refrain from asking questions so basic, so fundamental, so utterly commonsensical?

A: But, my friend, I am truly perplexed, truly lost.

B: There is no "why." Art and beauty simply can be separated — that is how the world operates. Or perhaps the world does not truly operate that way — but at the very least, people are firmly convinced that it does; at the very least, I am firmly convinced of it. How did our topic stray all the way here? Were we not speaking of tyrants? Are your associations not leaping rather too wildly?

A: My deepest apologies — no doubt asking you to aid me in this disputation shall prove a most difficult affair.

B felt a helpless resignation, and instinctively moved to take another sip of wine, only to discover that at some unnoticed moment the glass had already been drained.

B: With this character of yours — wandering daily through wild and errant musings — it is indeed hard for me to be of help. How about this: you buy me a drink, and I shall assist you all the more earnestly toward victory.

A: No problem at all — I truly do need your earnest assistance.

B: Then this drink you ordered — the one of many colors — is it truly good?

A: Not bad at all; I think you might give it a try.

B: No thank you — I shall order the same one as before.

A: Very well, my friend.


Mythos & Kallos · Part 11 · Tyrant δ

B pondered for a moment, then ordered another glass of the same wine as before.

Just at that moment, B heard a sound issuing from A's friend, who lay sunken in drunken slumber.

X: No… liar… I refuse to drink the cup that you drink… beautiful… false… all is drifting shadow… all is dream and phantasm… I simply will not drink the cup you ordered… nothing real at all… not good to drink… good to drink… not good to drink… the good-to-drink that is not good to drink… drinking the not-good-to-drink that is good to drink… anchor… wine… the anchor that is good to drink… the anchor that is not good to drink… put an anchor in the wine… I am not drunk… it is the anchor that is drunk… the sea is drunk too… the anchor got drunk… the anchor drank the anchor…

B had assumed that this friend of A's was finally about to awaken, only to find that X, having uttered these fragmentary, incoherent drunken words, lapsed once more into oblivion, sinking again into quiet sleep.

B: I did not expect that your friend would not only be fair of face, but possess so pleasing a voice as well.

A: Oh, indeed — X is a person of exceeding charm.

B: X? What a peculiar name — I have never heard anyone called by such a name. Its pronunciation is most curious, and pleasing to the ear.

A: That is not X's original name, but X's stage name. According to X, it is a name belonging to the drama (δρᾶμα = drama), and X dislikes being addressed by the original, true name.

B: Your friend has quite the character. Then do you know X's true name?

A: X did once tell me the true name — yet that recollection reminds me of a most perplexing experience.

B: What experience?

A: X always delights in casting forth conundrums I cannot resolve, plunging me into bewilderment and helplessness.

B: Oh?

A: My friend, after X spoke the true name to me, X hurled at me a question which to this very day I cannot answer. X asked me: though I had now come to know a "true name," how could I be certain I had not been deceived? Perhaps this so-called "true name" was merely another "stage name" — another name belonging to the stage, another name fashioned for artistic creation.

B: Surely that was a jest between friends? It seems you two are on good terms.

A: Oh, though X is indeed my friend, when X spoke those words to me, X was considerably angry — with not the slightest trace of jesting.

B: How could that be? You enraged your friend?

A: Ah, my friend, why would you think so? I have no intention of enraging anyone.

B: Haha — perhaps those who are fair of face and sweet of voice are naturally more apt to be trusted? I cannot help feeling that your friend was the offended party. And judging by my own experience, those strange questions of yours truly do provoke impatience.

A: By the Gods above, these are questions whose answers I earnestly seek — never with the least intention of giving offense.

B: Then why was X angered at the time?

A: Oh, my friend, I too dearly wish to know the answer to that question. I remember only that after I recounted a certain myth, X's reaction grew fierce, and then X delivered unto me a long speech.

B: What did X say?

A: X said to me: X is a stage name; and since a person may possess a stage name, might not the stage name likewise possess a stage name of its own? They are, after all, merely names — all arbitrarily bestowed by humans. How, then, is a person ever to discern which one is the true name?

B: Wait — I am somewhat confused. What does it mean that "a stage name may possess its own stage name"?

A: Oh, I too asked X this very question. Facing it, X answered me: X is a stage name; X is a name (ὄνομα = name), and this name was arbitrarily bestowed by X's own self.

B: Hmm, this much I understand — and then?

A: X said that a stage name may endlessly repeat this process of arbitrary naming; a stage name may have its own stage name — this is to perform an "arbitrary naming" upon the "arbitrary naming of the stage name" once again. The name X may likewise have a name of its own: this is the name of a name, the X of X; the X of X may be Y, and the X of X may equally be Z.

B: This is far too confounding.

A: X explained that this is the naming of naming — or, one step further, the naming of the naming of naming; and such a process can be carried on in infinite regress.

B: I still do not understand. I can comprehend that "a name is an arbitrary human designation" — but why would anyone perform a naming upon a name? Is there any substantive meaning in doing so? To me this carries no practical significance whatsoever! Is it nothing but a pure game of thought?

A: My friend, I can understand how you feel.

B: We call an "apple" an "apple" — that is our naming of the "apple"; but we have no need to bestow a further name upon "the name apple," nor would we ever do so — and thus there exists in this world no such thing as "the name of the name of the apple."

B noticed that A had drifted off slightly.

B: What are you pondering now?

A: Oh — some strange images just flashed across my mind.

B: What images?

A: I seemed to behold a golden apple smiling at me. It spoke to me gently: "My dear good companion, you have always called me the golden apple — but that is merely the stage name I took for your sake; my real name you are unable to translate. Now, do you wish to ask what my name truly is? My friend — is that not the very question I have been asking you all along? What is your name?"

B: Good heavens — I do believe you are drunk as well. You are not about to collapse upon the table too, are you?

A: My apologies, my friend — I am not certain whether I am drunk. Perhaps I am already drunk. I seem unable now to escape the golden apple's question; I keep turning over these questions — "what is your name," "what is my name."

B looked pained with exasperation.

B: Stop thinking about it! You cannot let your thoughts leap about like this! Wait — you are driving me mad as well. What were we speaking of before?

A: It was this: if an apple has a name, does the name of that apple possess a name of its own?

B: It does not! The name of the apple has no name. If you ask an apple what its name is, the apple will tell you it is called an apple; but if you ask an apple "what name does its name bear," it will likely stare at you as one stares at a fool, deeming you an exceptionally stupid piece of fruit!

A: But, my friend, I am no fruit!

B burst into laughter.

B: It is a relief that you can still remember you are not a fruit. Might we now return to some more ordinary topic?

A: Oh, my friend — I seem to have found the clue as to why the image of the golden apple came into my mind.

B: I hope this time your associations lead to nothing more outlandish.

A: I promise they shall not. I have at last remembered by what myth my friend X was so enraged; in that myth there is indeed a golden fruit.

B: What myth?

And so A began recalling for B that myth concerning the golden fruit.

Long, long ago, upon a beautiful little island, there lived a loving husband and wife, and unto them a child was born.

They gave thanks to the Gods for granting them new life, and gave thanks to the Gods for watching over their love.

Yet this loving couple could not, for the longest time, decide what their newborn child should be called.

They thought of many names — thinking and thinking, and thinking again.

They had assumed that once the child was born, the name would settle itself; yet a whole month had passed since the birth, and still the child's name remained undecided.

One day, as they played with the newborn babe, they said to the infant who knew only how to cry: "Our child, what would you wish to be called? What would you wish to become in days to come? By what would you wish to be known among people? Our little darling — you know only how to cry; shall we simply call you the Little Crybaby?"

The couple's idle words of teasing, beyond all expectation, became reality; from that day forth the newborn child never ceased from weeping, crying for fully three days and three nights.

Though the little babe still appeared perfectly healthy, this unnatural phenomenon plunged the couple into panic. And so they resolved to seek the aid of the Gods, hoping the Gods might cause their child to cease its weeping.

They prepared offerings for the Gods (θυσία = thysia = offering, sacrifice), and bearing the child, they sailed by boat to the little island where the temple stood. Having moored their vessel, they prayed within the temple with sincere hearts.

It seemed the Gods had hearkened unto the couple's prayer — for that very night, they dreamed a dream (ὄνειρος = dream).

In the dream, a flying fruit (καρπός = fruit / result) radiant with golden light asked them, smiling: "Since you two love one another, surely you must know my real name. What is my name?"

The husband and wife guessed many answers, yet none was correct.

Among these answers were "the Golden Apple," "the Shining Apple," "the Precious Apple," "the Golden Shining Apple," "the Precious Shining Apple," and so on.

The golden fruit said to them: "Those, perhaps, are my names within the drama (δρᾶμα = drama). I am very fond of these names (ὄνομα = onoma = name, reputation) — but what is my real name? What fruit (καρπός = fruit / result) am I, in truth?"

Within the dream the couple guessed many times more, yet never once did they guess aright.

And so, even within the dream, anxiety seized them.

And in their anxiousness, they awoke.

Perhaps the Gods had truly intervened in their plight — for upon waking they found the child had ceased to cry. They beheld the little babe embracing a golden fruit, sleeping soundly and in peace.

And thus their child was given the name "the Golden Apple".

B: That is all? What a short myth. Now this I cannot understand, my friend.

A: My friend, what is it you do not understand?

B: I do not understand why X would be enraged by this myth of yours.

A: I too have been pondering this very question.

B: Think again — did you say nothing else?

A: Oh — now I remember. This myth has a latter half.

B wore an expression of considerable resignation, took a sip of wine, and gestured for A to continue.

Many, many more years passed, and the child who had been named "the Golden Apple" grew to adulthood.

One day, upon another small island near home, this child met an old man.

The elder and the younger fell into conversation.

Old Man: Child, what is your name?

Child: I am called the Golden Apple.

The old man seemed greatly surprised that the child bore such a name, and asked after its origin; the child thereupon recounted to the old man the story that the parents had lived through.

To the child's surprise, upon hearing it the old man could not stop laughing.

Child: Old sir, I do not understand — is my name truly so laughable?

Old Man: Forgive me, child, truly I did not mean to laugh — I simply could not restrain myself.

Child: I do not understand.

Old Man: The fault is mine. In my homeland, the people all love to cultivate fruit; the growing of fruit has even become a formal contest, and each year the sweetest fruit is offered up to the Gods.

Child: Oh.

Old Man: This cultural custom of my homeland has even shaped the naming of children. Just as my own parents named me "the Sweetness of Heaven". But I do not like this name in the least. Good heavens, child — you can scarcely imagine how much mockery this name has brought upon me from childhood to this very day.

Child: Oh.

Old Man: In my homeland, the name of the sweetest fruit is "apple" — and that is the origin of your name.

Child: So that is how it was?

The old man seemed filled with remorse.

Old Man: Yes. And yet, child — in truth, your homeland possesses no real apples at all.

Child: What?

Old Man: In my youth, during one tedious voyage, I conceived a prank. I brought the least sweet, the most bland and flavorless fruit of my homeland to these small islands, and planted it here. When the people of these isles asked me what fruit this was, I told them: this is a golden fruit called the apple, and it is exceedingly sweet. The people here believed it, and came to call this unsweet, near-flavorless fruit the apple.

Child: What?

Old Man: Yet in this world there is no golden apple — and that fruit was never sweet at all.

Child: What? So — the fruit I have eaten all my life is not called the apple?

Old Man: I am deeply sorry; I never imagined it would touch your life. In my youth I meant only to play a jest.

Child: But these fruits truly are very sweet.

Old Man: Child, that is precisely the crux of it. Never in all my reckoning did I imagine that this fruit, once planted in your homeland, would turn sweet. I believe this is truly a prank (δόλος = dolos = prank, mischief) played upon me by the Goddesses of Fate (Μοῖραι) — the Fates' mockery of me. You can scarcely imagine how astounded I was when the people here told me the fruit was truly sweet.

Child: Old sir, then I wish to know — what is the real name of this golden fruit?

Old Man: Why do you wish to know?

The child looked at the old man most earnestly, and said with utter sincerity: Because I wish to know my real name — what I am truly called.

The old man pondered, and knew not how to answer the child's question.

The old man did not wish the child to change names on account of this.

Old Man: Child, in your homeland, this sweet golden fruit is called the apple — the Golden Apple does exist.

The child sank into bewilderment.

B saw that A had halted once more, falling silent.

B: So what, in the end, is that golden fruit called?

A: I have forgotten — I have forgotten the content of the myth that follows.

B: I believe I am about to grow as angry as X. You cannot tell half a story and then forget the rest!

A: My deepest apologies, my friend.
