After B finished speaking, a voice rose in objection — yet it came neither from A nor from C.

A was seated in a dim corner of the bar. When B caught sight of A, A was lifting a glass, sipping lightly at a cocktail that seemed monotonous from afar, yet up close revealed itself richly colored and layered.

Though A was drinking alone, A was not without company; beside A slumped a person deep in drunken sleep.

B: Hey, what are you doing here? Is this your friend? May I sit here?

Though for most of the time I merely listened, offering nothing of insight, my companion was clearly delighted at having performed beyond the ordinary in the arts of debate tonight. I remained the beleaguered defender, barely holding the line; and just as my friend was about to seize the decisive victory, perhaps Heaven took pity on ignorant and helpless me — the God of Sleep (Ὕπνος = Hypnos) descended and delivered me.

Yet my friend is practicing a certain art (τέχνη = techne) that seems to me quite terrifying; this terrifying art (τέχνη = techne) can make the unreasonable appear reasonable, and likewise make the reasonable appear unreasonable. This friend of mine can argue a proposition from the affirmative side or the negative side; but whether affirming or negating, my friend must always emerge the victor — and I, alas, have become the sparring dummy.

B: Haha, your friend is truly amusing. Though I do not believe such an art truly exists in this world — for truth cannot be concealed — I suspect our conversation will prove most interesting. And now I feel somewhat disinclined to help you, for to prove that "the tyrant is beautiful" might indeed be a fascinating thought experiment.

Amid the clash of attack and defense, my mind stood empty; I could only struggle to recall a myth (μῦθος = myth) I had mostly forgotten, yet I could not fashion it into effective speech.

Long, long ago, a person searching through old archives found, in a dust-laden records room, a file concerning an old man who had gone mad in his final years. The documents, stacked together, were remarkably thick, covered with annotations in the handwriting of many different people; these annotations were so numerous and so tangled that the archive-searcher was seized by a feeling of having nowhere to begin.

After a period of sorting and disentangling, the archive-searcher came to understand, from the various fragments, the life of this deranged old man, and compiled it into a set of notes, adding it to that mountain of archives already swollen beyond measure.

The deranged old man had once held high office — a core figure of a regime. He could have passed peacefully toward life's end amid splendor, wealth, and a house full of children and grandchildren; yet for certain reasons, in his late years he defected alone from his own country, fleeing to an enemy nation to seek asylum.

Even though he had once stood at the core of power, grasping the levers of the machinery of state, he could not take his family with him; this he could not accomplish.

After his defection, his family suffered immense calamity on account of his treason: some died, some went mad, some lived lives worse than death.

The old man had known, before his defection, that these consequences would inevitably follow, but in his despair he believed he had no other choice. His purpose was one alone: to overthrow the regime he had once served in loyalty. He himself, of course, could not accomplish this single-handedly; he could only place his hopes in the enemy regime to fulfill his wish. The enemy of one's enemy may not be a friend — but is at least the enemy of one's enemy.

He believed he had a righteous cause, yet he could not suppress the profound guilt he bore toward his family, his students, and his friends.

He knew he had ruined the wife who had accompanied him through so many years. Before departing, he breathed not half a word to her; he kept his secret from the one closest to him and most trusted, for he did not know whether his home lay under some more hidden surveillance.

He knew he had ruined those children of his who had been destined to share the fruits of the established order; he knew he had ruined his grandchildren, not yet grown. His descendants, even if they did not die, would sink to the political bottom of society.

He knew he had ruined his students: ruined those students overflowing with talent, ruined those students whose hearts still held naive ideals, ruined those students who, in a society starved of trust, had still been willing to trust him.

He knew he had ruined the friends bound to him by affection — whether allies of interest or intimates of the heart.

He knew he had ruined many others besides, but he did not know their names; he knew only that those nameless people had been misled by him, deceived by him.

This remorse, like a tsunami, tormented him without ceasing, and in his solitary dwelling the old man's mind grew ever more unstable.

The government sheltering the old man therefore decided that — beyond the routine surveillance-protection and medical personnel — under the pretext of assisting the old man in writing his autobiography, an additional person would be assigned specifically to converse with him. The government's analysts judged that this might ease the old man's mental affliction.

This task could only be undertaken by one whose political loyalty was beyond question.

Yet no one was willing to take the assignment. The work carried no political significance whatsoever, and no one wished to face, day and night, a mentally unsound defector-official whose symbolic political value far exceeded his actual worth.

And so, a person who had chosen the losing side in an internal political struggle was assigned to carry out this meaningless task.

This unlucky soul's codename in the archives was R005.

On the very night R005 learned of the new assignment, R005 sank into a still deeper depression, unable to sleep even after taking medication.

Yes — R005, who had picked the losing faction, likewise suffered from severe mental illness, and had long been under psychiatric treatment.

A friend of R005, likewise politically marginalized for backing the wrong side, upon hearing of this, said in jest: such things are truly fitting for our country — the mentally ill treating the mentally ill, the mentally ill managing the mentally ill, the mentally ill ruling the mentally ill. No novel could ever imagine a plot of reality that so transcends mundane logic; politicians are the true artists.

R005 wrote in the notes that R005 had once seen this old man on television. But truthfully, when meeting the real man face to face, R005 could recognize him as the same person only by his outline. The old man before R005 bore not the slightest trace of a high official of weighty power; he seemed rather a terminal cancer patient undergoing chemotherapy — the corrupting flesh sliding inevitably toward life's end, able only, in extreme agony and utter helplessness, to delay the coming of Death (Θάνατος = Thanatos).

R005 recorded: in all fairness, those cynical old men in the park looked a hundred times better than he did.

Scattered about the old man's room were scraps of paper covered in chaotic scrawls. R005 could not discern what logic this well-educated old man was trying to express in that heap of fragments; what R005 saw was only boundless madness.

R005 told the friend: this is what a true madman's room looks like — compared with this, I am still quite normal, quite healthy.

When R005 met the old man, the television in his room was playing some childish children's cartoons; the old man's mind was plainly not on the screen — perhaps he merely wished for some sound in the room.

Old Man: Hello — might you be the one who is to help me write my autobiography?

R005: Yes, sir. I am honored to take part in this work; we are fortunate to glimpse the history you have witnessed.

Old Man: Thank you. My sinful life holds little worth telling. The government wishes me to write an autobiography of myself, and I am boundlessly grateful — yet I do not know where to begin. My mind is flooded with remorse. I persist in living rather than taking my own life because I hope to see the day that regime collapses. Though I know this will not happen within my remaining years, still I harbor this hope — it is the sole reason by which I persuade myself to go on living. Perhaps if I live one more day, I might yet see it? I know I deceive myself — but what if, by some small chance, such a miracle truly occurs?

R005: Sir, the miracle will come. Life always holds hope.

Old Man: Miracle… I do not believe in miracles… but I hope the miracle comes…

Then, suddenly, the old man burst into uncontrollable weeping.

Old Man: Miracle?! Miracle?! Will a miracle truly come?! Teacher deceived me! Teacher deceived me!

R005 was instantly at a loss, utterly unable to comprehend why the old man, conversing calmly one moment, had emotionally collapsed the next.

R005 tried to do something, and steeling all nerve, asked: What teacher? Sir, who is this teacher you speak of?

Old Man: The painter! The painter! The painter who passed by my home! I do not know Teacher's name… he was a painter… when I was still small, he taught me some things, and I called him Teacher. He told me… that miracles exist.

R005: What miracle?

Old Man: He said he had once been a very, very bad man, and had now become not so bad — and that this was a miracle. He deceived me — miracles do not exist!

The old man's emotions spiraled wholly out of control, and the conversation could proceed no further.

Subsequent analysis concluded that the word "miracle" was the key trigger of the old man's emotional reaction, and it was recommended that R005 henceforth avoid raising any concept related to "miracle." Since the old man was fluent in many languages, the government's analysts even conducted, specifically for each language the old man knew, a certain etymological tracing of the word "miracle."

The analysts advised R005 to memorize these etymological connections, which might prove useful in future conversations.

Gazing at the dense etymological materials in hand — thick as a page of magic spells — R005 felt only a crawling of the scalp.

Only at that moment did R005 remember that this old man — emotionally shattered, weeping like a six-year-old child — had once been a core figure of the enemy nation's educational system and propaganda apparatus; that this old man's education exceeded that of most members of the team; that he himself had once been the teacher of countless people, with countless students under his instruction.

And that the old man had survived to this day proved, too, that he possessed a singular discernment in worldly affairs and political alignment — otherwise he would long ago have been eliminated in the many political purges.

R005 thought: this truly is an absurd world.

At this point, B, listening to A speak, could not help but interrupt.

B: Wait — this is not a myth, surely?

A: Why do you say so? My friend, this is a myth (μῦθος = myth).

B: This is a story, not a myth. Its content is far too modern — utterly incongruous with the word "myth"; and besides, there are no Gods in this story. A story without Gods cannot be called a myth.

A: Ah, my friend, I think there is a certain reason in what you say. But this myth contains the concept of "miracle (θαῦμα)" — might it perhaps just barely count as a myth (μῦθος = myth)?

B: "Once a very bad man, now become not so bad — this is a miracle" — this counts as a miracle? A miracle must be an awe visible to the naked eye — the moving of mountains and overturning of seas, the changing of heaven and remaking of earth — beyond the reach of human power, surpassing imagination. That is what is called a "miracle."

A appeared deeply troubled, deeply torn.

B: Never mind. If you insist on naming this story a "myth," then let it be as you wish. For my part, I hold that the concepts of "modern", "story" and "myth" require a certain demarcation; they must not blur into one another — there must be clear boundaries between them.

A: Why so?

B: It unsettles me, and it makes me uncomfortable.

A: I had not thought this would offend you, my friend.

B: It is nothing. Pray continue with this "myth", friend.

Only after many sessions with the old man did R005 laboriously piece together the figure of the "Painter-Teacher" of whom the old man spoke.

Yet the old man's narrations contained contradictions between earlier and later tellings, and the reality of this "Painter-Teacher" could not be verified. The old man's psychological state cast doubt on whether he could lucidly maintain the boundary between the real and the illusory; the "Painter-Teacher" was very possibly nothing but a product woven of the old man's chaotic memories and imaginings.

Even so, R005 continued — soothing the old man to steady his emotions on the one hand, while on the other recording, fragment by fragment, the old man's portrait of the "Painter-Teacher."

R005 complained to the friend: why does my psychiatrist do nothing but prescribe pills? R005 felt more like a practicing psychotherapist than the licensed psychologists — and yet R005 was in fact a patient who had to take medication on schedule. What a mad world this truly is.

R005's friend replied: perhaps your psychiatrist is also taking the very pills they prescribe.

In the old man's "memories," when he was ten, or eleven, or perhaps twelve years old, a stranger (ξένος = xenos = stranger) came to the little town where his family lived; the stranger seemed to be a painter who traveled and sketched as he went.

The old man felt that the stranger's paintings were in truth nothing remarkable, but the stories he told were captivating. And so the children of the town would gather from time to time around the painter, delighting to hear his tales.

One day, the old man (then still a child) saw the painter painting beside a small stream.

Old Man (Child): What are you painting?

Painter: I am painting the spirals in the water.

Old Man (Child): It's not pretty.

Painter: I do not think it is pretty either.

Old Man (Child): If it's not pretty, why do you still paint it?

Painter: I am still learning.

Old Man (Child): How many years have you been learning to paint?

Painter: A good many years now.

Old Man (Child), giggling: You're so stupid — learning for years and still haven't learned it.

Painter: Indeed, I have no particular gift for painting.

Old Man (Child): Then why do you still paint?

Painter: One must always be learning — even the things one is not skilled at, no? And you? Child, what are you good at?

Old Man (Child): Mathematics. The teachers at school all say I calculate very fast — that I have a real gift for mathematics.

Painter: How impressive. So do you wish to become a mathematician when you grow up?

Old Man (Child): I don't know.

Painter: Have you no dream?

Old Man (Child): Dream?

Painter: Yes, a dream. Or, to put it another way: is there some question you have kept pondering ever since you were very small?

Old Man (Child): No, not really. Do you have one?

Painter: Me? I do. Ever since I was small, I have wanted to know what a miracle (θαῦμα = thauma = miracle, wonder, marvel) is.

Old Man (Child): That's so silly — miracles don't exist at all!

Painter: Why?

Old Man (Child): The teacher at school said so.

Painter: And what else did the teacher at school say?

Old Man (Child): Teacher said we should study science and engineering well, and then…

Painter: And then what?

Old Man (Child): And then drive out the foreigners who bully us!

Painter: Your teacher is very bold indeed.

Old Man (Child): Are you afraid of the foreigners too?

Painter: Our nation has perished, and foreigners whose language and culture differ from ours now rule over us — yes, I too am afraid.

Old Man (Child): Right? Our teacher says the same.

Painter: So your dream is to become a mathematician or scientist, and then drive out the foreigners?

Old Man (Child): I don't know — maybe. Though I calculate fast, I don't seem all that interested in mathematics.

Painter: Oh? Then what question does interest you?

Old Man (Child): One day, our teacher showed us some photographs and said this man was a famous tyrant (τύραννος = tyrant) in history, and told us not to imitate him — that we must be good people.

Painter: And then?

Old Man (Child): But… when I looked at that picture, I thought the tyrant looked very handsome — not like a bad man at all.

Painter: Is that so?

Old Man (Child): And then I thought: every person was once a baby, and teacher says babies are all innocent and pure. So I wondered — how does an innocent, pure person become a tyrant, a bad man? I asked teacher this question, and teacher said: if you learn bad things you become a bad person, and looking handsome does not mean he or she is good.

Painter: Oh?

Old Man (Child): Teacher gave me an answer, but from time to time I still can't help wondering: why does a baby become a tyrant?

Painter: A most interesting question indeed.

Old Man (Child): And you? Have you found your miracle?

Painter: Heh heh — I have found it.

Old Man (Child): I don't believe you.

Painter: Why do you not believe me?

Old Man (Child): Teacher says there are no miracles in this world — only science and human effort. The true miracle is science.

Painter: Is that so.

Old Man (Child): So you should stop believing in miracles!

Painter: But I truly have found a miracle.

Old Man (Child): I don't believe you. What did you find?

Painter: I was once a very, very bad man, and now I have become not so bad — this is a miracle.

Old Man (Child): Liar! That's not a miracle!

Painter: But this is a miracle.

Old Man (Child): That's not a miracle. I don't believe you.

Painter: I am not deceiving you — I do not deceive children.

Old Man (Child): Then prove it to me.

Painter: Think of it this way: do you not find little babies terribly noisy? They only cry — cry, then eat, eat, then sleep, and wake to cry again.

Old Man (Child): Yes, little babies are very annoying.

Painter: And then, slowly, the little baby becomes less annoying — no longer merely crying all day, but responding to its parents. From knowing only itself, to knowing that there are others in the world, knowing how to answer others, engaging in ever more meaningful exchange with those beyond itself.

Old Man (Child): Mm.

Painter: Do you play with children a few years old? Do you find them very annoying?

Old Man (Child): Yes, children a few years old are also very annoying.

Painter: But not as annoying as little babies?

Old Man (Child): Not that annoying, I suppose — but I still won't play with them; they're stupid.

Painter: This is the miracle, child!

Old Man (Child): That's just normal growing up. You're lying.

Painter: Oh, child — perhaps one day you will discover that I have not deceived you. That a human being grows — this fact in itself is a miracle (θαῦμα). That a person grows is a thing of wonder; and that which awakens wonder is a miracle (θαῦμα).

Old Man (Child): You really aren't lying to me?

Painter: I truly am not lying to you.

Old Man (Child): I'll go back and ask teacher.

Painter: You trust your teacher very much.

Old Man (Child): Teacher is very good to us — they even gives us snacks.

Painter: How lovely.

Old Man (Child): Then let me ask you: does a person keep growing forever?

Painter: Oh, that is by no means certain.

Old Man (Child): What happens to a person who does not grow?

Painter: Perhaps you might imagine it thus: a child of several years who still, like a little baby, cries then eats, eats then sleeps, sleeps then wakes, and wakes only to eat again?

Old Man (Child): How frightening — does such a thing really happen?

Painter: Oh, it is quite common.

Old Man (Child): You're lying — I've never seen it.

Painter: I am not deceiving you.

Old Man (Child): Are there really such people in this world? Are they sick with some disease?

Painter: There are, child. They suffer from a disease of wishing to freeze Time (Χρόνος = Chronos = Time); they hope thereby to substitute it for Eternity (Αἰών = Aion = Eternality), and to live out their days in a paradise where one never grows up, until life's very end.

Old Man (Child): Does such a disease really exist?

Painter: It truly does.

Old Man (Child): Is this disease hard to cure?

Painter: I am not sure myself. Would you like to become a doctor?

Old Man (Child): Teacher says becoming a doctor is very hard.

Painter: Being a doctor is very dangerous work — the doctor may well be infected by the patient; it takes a measure of courage (ἀνδρεία = andreia = bravery) to be equal to it.

Old Man (Child): Can't the doctor just be careful?

Painter: Oh, or you might imagine a situation such as this: the patient is very beautiful to behold, and — like all who gaze upon beautiful things — the doctor cannot resist reaching out to touch the diseased patient; and thus the doctor stands in danger of infection.

Old Man (Child): Is that sort of thing common?

Painter: Very common indeed.

Old Man (Child): How terrifying — I don't want to be a doctor anymore.

Painter: Then what do you want to be?

Old Man (Child): I don't know. Do you have any suggestions? What do you think I should be?

Painter: Child, you and I have something in common. Perhaps you could come and learn this work that I am doing?

Old Man (Child): A painter?

Painter: I am not a painter.

Old Man (Child): Huh? You're not a painter — then what are you?

Painter: I am one who pursues miracles (θαῦμα = thauma).

Old Man (Child): Does pursuing miracles count as a job?

Painter: I suppose so? I am not certain myself.

Old Man (Child): Does this job have a name?

Painter: Oh — I seem to recall a saying, though sadly I have forgotten its source. The saying goes roughly thus: people discovered the traces of miracle (θαῦμα) within myth (μῦθος = myth); people marveled in wonder at the miracle (θαῦμα), and so people became lovers of myth (μῦθος = myth); and the lovers of myth are forever in pursuit of the miracle (θαῦμα).

Old Man (Child): Doesn't sound like much.

Painter: I think so too.
