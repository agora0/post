微故事: 等待老人的笑聲 (東加豆)

(今天無法上載圖片)

我叫阿志，是一位大笑瑜伽導師，我的證書上寫著 

"Professional"，我不否認的，因為我已經習慣精準掌握大家的節奏、笑聲、眼神、氣氛等等。課堂上，我吐出的一字一句，彷彿像一首熟悉的旋律，大家像每一粒音符。但回到家，一切都失控了。

家裡那隻老金屬茶壺，水滾得極慢，每次我趕著倒水，它偏像跟我作對，半天才發出微弱而拖沓的（咕嚕……咕嚕……）。

比茶壺更慢的，是老爸老媽。老爸倒一杯水，手抖得像在彈奏慢板樂曲；老媽講一件鄰居小事，講了十分鐘還停在開頭。我心裡焦躁，語氣就忍不住帶點冷，其實我看得見，兩老退化得快。那種心酸，卡在心裡。我可以對任何人笑，卻對等一壺水滾毫無耐性。後來，我乾脆專攻老人大笑，為他們服務。

午後的長者康體中心，坐著二十多位動作遲緩的老人。有些帶著輕度失智，單純的幽默與笑話對他們而言過於抽象。正如筆記所言，隨著大腦細胞退化，長者理解幽默的能力會下降，所以我們不靠笑話，只靠身體帶動。

我走進圈子裡，帶領著最基礎的（拍手與呼喚）——我們不喊 "Hoho Hahaha"，而是刻意放慢節奏，改為輕柔的 "Ho... Ho... Ha... Ha..."。這正是大笑瑜伽的好處，它不需要任何心智能力，純粹透過呼吸與動作增加氧氣，讓長者也能受益。

那一刻，看著老人跟著我抖動雙肩、艱難卻專注地發出聲音，我突然愣住了。那頻率，那微弱而微小的震動，竟與我家那隻老茶壺水沸前的（咕嚕聲）完全重合。茶壺不是壞了，老人們也不是故意拖延。那緩慢的醞釀，不過是為了在某一瞬間，頂開那沉重的壺蓋。

我走出機構，街上的陽光有些晃眼。回到家，老爸正慢吞吞地拿著茶壺，手微微顫抖。

我看著昨天那個還在催促他們的自己…

我站在旁邊，聽著那微弱的咕嚕聲，我第一次覺得，那咕嚕聲，像極了課堂上最動聽的 Ho Ho Ha Ha，一下一下，從橫隔膜震出來。

完


Micro-story: Waiting for Seniors's Laughter  (by Tonkabean)


My name is Ah Chi, and I’m a Laughter Yoga teacher. My certificate says "Professional." I don’t deny it, because I’m used to precisely guiding everyone's rhythm, laughter, eye contact, and energy. In class, every word I say is like a familiar melody, and everyone is a note in it. But when I go home, everything falls out of control.

The old metal kettle at home takes forever to boil. Every time I’m in a hurry to pour water, it seems to go against me, making only a faint, slow gurgle... gurgle... after a long wait.

Slower than the kettle are my parents. When Dad pours water, his hands shake as if he’s playing a slow musical piece. When Mom tells a story about a neighbor, she stays at the beginning even after ten minutes. Impatience grows inside me, and my tone turns cold. Truth is, I can see them aging quickly, and that heartache gets stuck in my chest. I can smile at anyone, yet I have zero patience waiting for a kettle to boil. Later, I decided to focus entirely on leading Laughter Yoga for seniors.

In the afternoon at the senior center, over twenty slow-moving elders sit in a circle. Some have mild dementia; simple humor and jokes are too abstract for them. Just as the notes say, as brain cells age, seniors lose their capacity to understand humor. So we don't rely on jokes, but on physical movement instead.

I step into the circle to lead the most basic clapping and chanting. We don't shout "Ho Ho Ha Ha Ha," but intentionally slow down the rhythm to a soft "Ho... Ho... Ha... Ha..." This is the beauty of Laughter Yoga—it requires no mental effort, purely boosting oxygen through breathing and movement so that seniors can benefit too.

At that moment, watching them shake their shoulders and try their best to make a sound, I suddenly freeze. That rhythm, those tiny vibrations, match the gurgle of my old kettle right before it boils. The kettle isn't broken, and the elders aren't delaying on purpose. That slow brewing is just gathering steam to push open the heavy lid.

I walk out of the center, the sunlight on the street a little blinding. Back home, Dad is slowly holding the kettle, his hand shaking slightly.

I look at myself from yesterday—the one who was always rushing them...

I stand beside him, listening to the faint gurgling sound. 

"For the first time, I felt that the gurgle sounded just like the most beautiful 'Ho Ho Ha Ha' from my class—vibrating up, beat by beat, right from the diaphragm."

End
